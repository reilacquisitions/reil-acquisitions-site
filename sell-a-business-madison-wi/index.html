Sell Your Business in Madison, Wisconsin

Confidential valuation, blind marketing and buyer representation for Dane County business owners considering an exit — from first conversation to final payment.

If you own a business in Madison or anywhere in Dane County and you are starting to think about an exit, you are in a stronger position than you probably realise. Madison is one of the most stable mid-size economies in the Midwest, and buyers actively look for companies here.

What most owners lack is not a buyer. It is a process — a valuation they trust, a confidential way to reach qualified buyers, and someone negotiating on their side. That is the work we do.

Why Madison Businesses Attract Serious Buyers

Dane County is not a one-industry market, and that diversity is exactly what makes it attractive to acquirers. A buyer looking at a Madison company is not betting on a single employer or commodity.

  • As the state capital, Madison has a large and recession-resistant base of government, legal, education and professional-services demand.
  • The University of Wisconsin–Madison supplies a steady stream of engineering and life-sciences talent, and has spun out a long list of companies in diagnostics, lab services and health technology.
  • Health technology is a regional anchor. The concentration of health-software and laboratory employers around Madison and Verona has created a deep pool of experienced technical staff and a network of suppliers that serve them.
  • Precision manufacturing and machining in and around Dane County serve medical devices, food processing and industrial OEMs — sectors where contracts are long and switching costs are high.
  • Population growth. Dane County has consistently been one of Wisconsin's fastest-growing counties, which supports commercial services, construction, facility maintenance, healthcare practices and consumer businesses.
  • Agribusiness and food. Wisconsin's dairy, food-processing and agricultural technology base is right at the doorstep.

The practical effect: a well-run Madison company with clean books will draw interest from individual buyers, regional operators and private equity groups at the same time. Your job is to run a competitive process so those buyers bid against each other rather than against you.

What Your Madison Business Is Worth

Almost every small and mid-sized business in Wisconsin is valued on one of two earnings measures.

Seller's Discretionary Earnings (SDE)

SDE is the total financial benefit one owner-operator receives: pre-tax profit, plus the owner's compensation and perks, plus depreciation, interest and one-time expenses. It answers the question, "what would a single owner-operator actually take out of this business?" It is the standard measure for smaller, owner-dependent companies.

EBITDA

EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation, stated after paying a market-rate manager. It is used when a business is large enough to run without the owner in the chair every day. Buyers and lenders generally require EBITDA for anything above roughly $1 million in earnings.

Typical ranges in the Madison market look like this — and they vary widely:

Business ProfileTypical Multiple
Owner-operated, under ~$2M revenue2–3× SDE
$2M–$10M revenue, owner still central3–5× SDE
$2M–$10M revenue, management in place4–6× EBITDA
$10M+ with recurring revenue and depth5–8× EBITDA

The multiple matters less than what moves it. In our experience these four factors swing a Wisconsin valuation more than the industry label does:

  • Customer concentration. If one customer is more than 20–25% of revenue, buyers discount hard or walk.
  • Owner dependence. If revenue depends on the owner's relationships or licence, risk goes up and the multiple comes down.
  • Revenue trend. Three years of growth is worth more than one strong year.
  • Recurring or contracted revenue. Service agreements, maintenance contracts and repeat institutional clients earn a premium.

The Wisconsin Factors That Affect Your Sale

Wisconsin has specific characteristics that change the maths on an exit, and they are easy to miss if you have only ever talked to a national broker.

Capital gains are taxed as ordinary income at the state level

Wisconsin does not give long-term capital gains a preferential rate the way the federal government does. For a Wisconsin seller, that makes how the transaction is structured — the allocation between goodwill, equipment, inventory, real property and any consulting or non-compete payments — materially more important than it would be in a state with a lower gains rate. A portion may also fall under the federal Net Investment Income Tax. This is planning you do before you sign anything, with your CPA involved.

There is no Wisconsin estate tax

Wisconsin has no state estate or inheritance tax, which simplifies succession planning for owners who intend to transfer a business within the family.

Manufacturing and agriculture credits

Wisconsin's manufacturing and agriculture credit can reduce the effective state tax burden on qualifying income. Whether it applies to your business, and how it interacts with a sale, is worth confirming with your accountant early.

SBA financing is widely available

Wisconsin has an active SBA lending community, including specialist acquisition lenders. SBA 7(a) loans support acquisitions up to $5 million with roughly 10% down, which means a large share of the local buyer pool depends on bank approval. A seller who can present clean, well-documented financials makes that approval easier — and a smoother financing process is often what separates a closed deal from a dead one.

Confidentiality is the norm

In a market the size of Madison, word travels. Most transactions here are handled confidentially through blind marketing, so employees, customers and competitors learn nothing until the owner chooses to disclose.

Who Buys Businesses in Madison

Understanding who is on the other side of the table changes how you position the company.

  • Individual buyers using SBA financing. Often leaving corporate roles, or experienced operators expanding. These buyers are price-sensitive but highly motivated, and they respond to businesses with clean books and transferable systems.
  • Strategic and add-on acquirers. Regional operators and private equity portfolio companies looking for a Madison foothold, a customer list or a capability they lack. These buyers usually pay the most because they can see synergies.
  • Private equity and search funds. Most active above roughly $1 million of EBITDA, where a professional management layer exists or can be built.
  • Family offices and high-net-worth individuals. Frequently less interested in a specific industry than in a durable, cash-generating Wisconsin business.
  • Employees and ESOPs. Occasionally the best outcome for culture and legacy, particularly for established Dane County companies with long-tenured staff.

How the Process Works

1. Confidential conversation

We listen first: why you are considering a sale, your timeline, what matters to you beyond price — staff, legacy, staying involved. If we are not the right fit, we say so and point you somewhere better.

2. Valuation and positioning

We review three years of financials and normalise earnings to establish defensible SDE or EBITDA. Then we build the story that answers a buyer's real questions: why is revenue durable, why will it continue without you, and what is the growth path.

3. Blind marketing

Your business is presented anonymously — sector, size, location, financial profile — to a targeted list of qualified buyers. You approve every release of information. Your name is never disclosed without your consent.

4. Buyer qualification and NDA

Interested buyers sign a Non-Disclosure Agreement and demonstrate proof of funds before receiving detail. This filters out the tire-kickers who waste months of an owner's time.

5. Letter of Intent and due diligence

We negotiate the LOI with you — price, structure, seller note, holdbacks, escrow, transition support. Then we manage diligence so that findings are answered rather than weaponised.

6. Structure and close

Asset versus stock, allocation, earn-outs, non-compete and consulting terms, and the transition plan. Your attorney and CPA lead the documents; we keep the deal moving so momentum is not lost.

How Long Does It Take to Sell a Business in Madison?

For most businesses in our range, six to twelve months from first conversation to money in the bank:

  • 2–4 months — preparation, valuation, positioning and marketing
  • 1–3 months — buyer interest, offers and negotiation to LOI
  • 60–90 days — due diligence, financing and closing

The variables that slow transactions down are almost always the same: financial records that do not reconcile, undeclared liabilities, one-customer dependency that only surfaces during diligence, or an unrealistic asking price that keeps qualified buyers away.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much is my Madison business worth?

Most owner-operated businesses sell on a multiple of SDE or EBITDA. Smaller companies typically trade at 2–3× SDE; businesses with $2M–$10M in revenue commonly reach 3–5× SDE or 4–6× EBITDA; larger firms with recurring revenue and management depth can reach 6–8× EBITDA. Customer concentration, owner dependence and revenue trend move the number more than industry does.

Do I need a broker to sell a business in Wisconsin?

No — you are not legally required to use one. But selling unrepresented usually means disclosing sensitive financials to unqualified buyers and negotiating without comparable market data. Most owners use an advisor for confidential marketing, buyer qualification and negotiation leverage.

How do I keep the sale confidential?

Through blind marketing. Buyers see an anonymised description first; identifying details are released only after a signed NDA and proof of funds. Employees, customers and competitors are contacted only with your approval.

What taxes will I owe when I sell in Wisconsin?

Wisconsin generally taxes capital gains as ordinary income, with no preferential long-term rate, and part of the gain may fall under the federal Net Investment Income Tax. Because transaction structure and asset allocation drive the total bill, this needs to be planned with your CPA before signing an LOI.

How do buyers finance an acquisition?

SBA 7(a) is the most common route for individual buyers, supporting deals up to $5 million with roughly 10% down. Above that, buyers use conventional bank debt, seller notes, mezzanine financing or private equity. Most Wisconsin deals include some seller financing.

Should I sell my real estate with the business?

Sometimes. Separating real property can attract more buyers — but it also changes your tax position and can remove a financing tool from the deal. In the Madison market both structures are common, and the right answer depends on the property, the buyer pool and your own plans.
